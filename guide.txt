=====================================================================
PART 1. THE DATA AND THE PROBLEM
=====================================================================

Slide 1.1  The data
  Script: exp.R, Figure 1  [MAIN]
  "Old Faithful waiting times": histogram + smooth density + rug.
  Table 1 (descriptive statistics) can sit next to it.

  What to say:
  - 272 waiting times between eruptions, 43 to 96 minutes,
    mean 70.9, sd 13.6.
  - The histogram has TWO humps (short waits around 55 min, long
    waits around 80). One normal distribution cannot describe this.
  - Idea: each eruption is of one of two hidden "types", each type
    has its own normal distribution. We do not observe the type.
    That is a 2-component Gaussian mixture:
        f(x) = pi1 * N(mu1, sigma1^2) + pi2 * N(mu2, sigma2^2)
  - Problem: if we knew the types, fitting would be trivial (mean and
    variance of each group). We do not know them -> this is exactly
    the "missing data" situation EM was built for.


=====================================================================
PART 2. HOW EM WORKS
=====================================================================

Slide 2.1  The algorithm in one picture
  Script: EM_step_by_step.R, Figure 1  [MAIN]
  "EM, step by step": six panels, the fit at iterations
  0, 16, 17, 19, 25 and 41 (converged).

  What to say (explain the two steps with this picture):
  - E-step: for every point, compute the probability that it belongs
    to each component, given the current fit. These are the dot
    colours: green = surely short wait, orange = surely long wait,
    grey = unsure.
  - M-step: refit each component as a WEIGHTED normal: weighted mean,
    weighted variance, and the total weight gives the mixing
    proportion.
  - Repeat until nothing changes.
  - Starting point: two almost identical components in the middle
    (means 70 and 72). In iteration 0 all dots are grey: EM does not
    know which point belongs where. By iteration 41 the two groups are
    cleanly separated.

Slide 2.2  The log-likelihood never goes down
  Script: EM_step_by_step.R, Figure 2  [MAIN]

  What to say:
  - The key theoretical guarantee of EM: every iteration increases
    (or keeps) the log-likelihood. The curve is never decreasing.
  - Interesting shape: for ~15 iterations almost nothing happens
    (log-likelihood stuck near -1095), then the two components split
    within about 10 iterations, then it flattens out at -1034.0.
    The flat start is because the starting point is close to a
    "saddle point" (see slide 4.1).


=====================================================================
PART 3. THE RESULT ON OLD FAITHFUL
=====================================================================

Slide 3.1  The fitted mixture
  Script: exp.R, Figure 2  [MAIN]  + Table 2
  Histogram, the two fitted components (dashed), their sum (solid),
  each point coloured by its most likely component.

  What to say:
  - Starting from k-means, EM converged in 12 iterations,
    log-likelihood = -1034.002.
  - Component 1 (short waits): pi = 0.361, mu = 54.6, sigma = 5.87
    Component 2 (long waits):  pi = 0.639, mu = 80.1, sigma = 5.87
  - About 36% of eruptions are followed by a short wait (~55 min),
    64% by a long wait (~80 min).

Slide 3.2  Is K = 2 the right number of components?
  Script: model_selection.R, Figure 1  [MAIN]  + Table 13
  "Choosing K for the Faithful data": AIC and BIC for K = 1..6,
  the minimum is circled.

  What to say:
  - More components always fit at least as well, so the
    log-likelihood alone would always pick the biggest K.
  - AIC and BIC add a penalty per parameter (3K - 1 parameters):
        AIC = -2 loglik + 2p,      BIC = -2 loglik + log(n) p
  - Both choose K = 2.
    K = 1 is far worse (AIC worse by 117, BIC by 106).
    AIC: K = 3 is a close second (only 1.3 worse).
    BIC: rejects K = 3 clearly (12 worse) because its penalty is
    larger (log 272 = 5.6 per parameter vs 2 for AIC).

  Script: model_selection.R, Figure 2  [BACKUP]
  "The two parts of AIC and BIC": the fit (-2 loglik) drops sharply
  from K = 1 to 2 and then is almost flat; the penalty keeps rising.
  Good if you want to explain WHY the curves in 3.2 turn back up.

  Script: model_selection.R, Figure 3  [MAIN or BACKUP]
  "Fitted mixtures with 1 to 4 components": K = 1 misses the two
  modes; K = 3 and 4 look almost the same as K = 2.
  Very intuitive picture - use it if you have time.

Slide 3.3  How precise are the estimates?
  Script: simulation_ci.R, Figure 1  [MAIN]  + Table 9
  "Faithful: parameter estimates with bootstrap intervals".

  What to say:
  - Bootstrap: resample the 272 points with replacement 500 times,
    refit EM every time, see how much the estimates move.
    SE = sd of the 500 estimates; 95% CI = 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles.
  - Results (estimate, 95% CI):
        pi1     0.361   [0.303, 0.432]
        mu1     54.6    [53.0, 56.0]
        mu2     80.1    [79.0, 81.1]
        sigma1  5.87    [4.84, 6.80]
        sigma2  5.87    [5.14, 6.82]
  - The means are known to about +-1 minute; the share of short
    waits is between about 30% and 43%.
  - (Part 5.4 checks whether these bootstrap intervals can be
    trusted - on Faithful the components are well separated, which
    is the case where they work well.)


=====================================================================
PART 4. PROPERTIES OF THE ALGORITHM (on the real data)
=====================================================================

Slide 4.1  Starting values matter
  Script: exp.R, Figure 3  [MAIN]  + Table 3
  Log-likelihood trajectories from six different starting points.

  What to say:
  - Four starts (good guess, k-means, wide & far, close means) all
    reach the SAME maximum, -1034.002, in 5 to 41 iterations.
  - S6 "saddle": both components start identical (same mean, weight,
    variance). Then both get exactly the same responsibilities, so they
    stay identical forever. EM "converges" after 2 iterations to
    -1095.3, which is just the one-normal fit. A saddle point is not a
    maximum, but EM cannot leave it.
  - S5 "unbalanced": one component with 2% weight and a tiny variance
    around x = 96. Its variance collapses towards 0 - the likelihood
    of a mixture can go to +infinity when one component sits on a
    single point. Our code detects this ("degenerate") and stops.
  - Lesson: EM only finds a LOCAL maximum (or gets stuck). In
    practice: run it from several starting points and keep the best
    (em_best does this: k-means start + 10 random starts).
  - Every run is monotone (Monotone = TRUE in Table 3).

Slide 4.2  EM converges linearly
  Script: convergence.R, Figure 1  [MAIN]  + Table 11
  "EM converges linearly": distance to the optimal parameters vs
  iteration, log scale.

  What to say:
  - Near the optimum, each iteration shrinks the remaining error by
    the same factor r:  error(t+1) ~ r * error(t).
    On a log scale that is a straight line - which is exactly what we
    see (dotted lines = slope implied by r).
  - On Faithful r = 0.658 from every start: each iteration removes
    about a third of the error, ~5.5 iterations per extra correct
    digit.
  - The lines are parallel: the speed near the end does not depend
    on where you started, only on the data (the optimum).
  - Note: Table 11 shows more iterations than Table 3 (e.g. k-means
    28 vs 12) because here the tolerance is much stricter (1e-14
    instead of 1e-8), to see the asymptotic behaviour.

  Script: convergence.R, Figure 2  [BACKUP]
  "Ratio of successive parameter steps": each step divided by the
  previous step. After some wiggles, every start settles at the same
  value 0.658 = r. Shows how r is measured.


=====================================================================
PART 5. SIMULATION STUDIES (when does EM work well?)
=====================================================================

Common set-up for all simulations (say this once):
  - Data generated from a KNOWN mixture:
        0.5 * N(0, 1) + 0.5 * N(delta, 1)
  - delta = distance between the two means. Both sds are 1, so
    delta = 0.5 means the components overlap almost completely,
    delta = 5 means they are clearly separate.
  - n = 500 points per dataset, many datasets per delta.
  - Because we know the truth, we can check how close EM gets.

Slide 5.1  Parameter recovery
  Script: simulation.R, Figure 1  [MAIN]  + Table 4
  "EM estimation error by component separation": estimate minus
  truth, boxplots per parameter and delta (R = 100 datasets).

  What to say:
  - delta = 3 and 5: errors are small and centred at 0 (bias ~0,
    RMSE of the means 0.07 - 0.14).
  - delta = 0.5 and 1.5: errors are huge (RMSE of mu2 = 1.05 at
    delta = 0.5, i.e. bigger than the separation itself). When the
    components overlap, the data contain very little information to
    tell them apart.
  - % non-monotone = 0 in every case: the log-likelihood never
    decreased in any of the 400 runs.

Slide 5.2  Classification
  Script: simulation.R, Figure 2  [MAIN]  + Table 5
  "EM classification error vs Bayes-optimal error".

  What to say:
  - Bayes error = the error of the best possible classifier that
    KNOWS the true parameters. Nothing can do better.
  - delta = 3 and 5: EM is practically optimal
    (7.4% vs 6.7%, 0.7% vs 0.6%).
  - delta = 1.5: EM 33.8% vs best possible 22.7%.
    delta = 0.5: 45.6% vs 40.1% (both close to coin-flipping).
  - Two extra findings from Table 5 that have no figure:
    * label swaps: at delta = 0.5, 18% of runs call the true first
      component "component 2" (12% at 1.5, 0% at 3 and 5).
    * mean ll gap ~ 2.9 at EVERY delta: the fitted model always has a
      HIGHER likelihood on the data than the true parameters. The
      MLE adapts to the noise in the sample (overfitting). Theory
      predicts roughly 2.5 for 5 free parameters (half a chi-square
      with 5 degrees of freedom), so ~2.9 is in line with it.

Slide 5.3  Speed depends on separation
  Script: convergence.R, Figure 3  [MAIN]  + Table 12
  "EM slows down when components overlap": rate r per dataset.

  What to say:
  - delta = 5: r = 0.43, ~15 iterations.
    delta = 3: r = 0.93, ~150 iterations.
    delta <= 2: r between 0.99 and 0.999 - hundreds to thousands of
    iterations per correct digit.
  - At delta = 0.5, 57% of runs had not converged after 5000
    iterations (Table 12, "% not converged").

  Script: convergence.R, Figure 4  [MAIN]
  "More label uncertainty = slower EM": rate vs average uncertainty
  of the class labels.

  What to say (this is the explanation):
  - When the E-step can tell which component each point belongs to
    (uncertainty near 0), each iteration makes big progress.
  - When points could belong to either component, each M-step learns
    little, and r goes to 1. Formally: r = the fraction of missing
    information.

  Script: convergence.R, Figure 5  [BACKUP]
  "Iterations needed": boxplots of the number of iterations.
  CAUTION: the runs that hit the 5000 limit are cut off at 5000, so
  for delta <= 1.5 the real numbers are even larger than shown.

  Script: simulation.R, Figure 3  [BACKUP]
  "Convergence speed by component separation": same message with the
  normal tolerance (median 968 iterations at delta = 0.5, 6 at 5).

  Script: simulation.R, Figure 4  [BACKUP]
  "Convergence status by component separation": share of runs that
  converged / degenerated / hit the limit. Almost all converge; 4% of
  runs at delta = 0.5 and 1% at 1.5 did not (mostly a collapsing
  component, "degenerate").

Slide 5.4  Can AIC/BIC find the right number of components?
  Script: model_selection.R, Figure 4  [MAIN]  + Table 14
  "Can AIC and BIC find the two components?": which K is chosen
  (true K = 2, green), R = 50 datasets per delta.

  Script: model_selection.R, Figure 5  [MAIN or BACKUP]
  "BIC is cautious, AIC is generous": % correct per criterion.

  What to say:
  - BIC chooses K = 1 in almost every dataset until delta = 2
    (correct only 0%, 0%, 2%, 12%), then 100% at delta = 3.
  - AIC finds K = 2 earlier (60% at delta = 2, 94% at 3) but picks
    too many components (K >= 3) in up to 30% of datasets.
  - Message: when two normals overlap strongly, their mixture really
    does look like ONE normal, so no criterion can detect the second
    component. This links to slide 5.1: if you cannot detect the
    component, you also cannot estimate it well.

Slide 5.5  Can the bootstrap intervals be trusted?
  Script: simulation_ci.R, Figure 2  [MAIN]  + Table 10
  "Do the bootstrap intervals cover the truth?": share of 95%
  intervals that contain the true value (R = 50 datasets, B = 100).

  What to say:
  - A 95% interval should contain the truth in 95% of datasets.
    The grey band is the noise expected from only 50 datasets
    (about +-6%).
  - delta = 3 and 5: coverage 86% - 100%, mostly inside the band.
    The bootstrap works.
  - delta = 0.5 and 1.5: coverage only 65% - 80%. The intervals are
    too narrow.

  Script: simulation_ci.R, Figure 3  [MAIN]
  "Size of bootstrap standard errors": bootstrap SE divided by the
  real variability of the estimates (ratio 1 = correct).

  What to say:
  - delta = 0.5 and 1.5: ratio 0.37 - 0.77, i.e. the bootstrap
    underestimates the uncertainty by up to a factor ~2.5.
  - delta = 3 and 5: ratio around 1 (0.8 - 1.2; the 1.49 for mu2 at
    delta = 5 is Monte Carlo noise from 50 datasets).
  - Likely reasons when components overlap:
    * every bootstrap refit starts at the original estimate, so it
      tends to stay near it;
    * 17 - 23% of the bootstrap refits stop at the iteration limit
      (1000) because EM is so slow there (slide 5.3) - they stop
      before moving far from the start;
    * the estimates are far from normally distributed (slide 5.1).
  - Honest conclusion: for well-separated components the bootstrap
    CIs are reliable; with strong overlap they are too optimistic.

  Script: simulation_ci.R, Figure 4  [BACKUP]
  "Every interval for Delta mu = 1.5": all 50 intervals sorted by the
  estimate; red = misses the truth. Makes the coverage idea concrete.


=====================================================================
PART 6. COMPARISON WITH OTHER METHODS
=====================================================================

The three methods (say this once):
  - EM (ours): the algorithm of this project.
  - mixtools::normalmixEM: the standard published EM for mixtures in
    R. Important discovery: with its default settings it does NOT run
    plain EM but ECM (Expectation / Conditional Maximization): in each
    iteration it updates weights and means, then does a SECOND E-step,
    then updates the variances. So each iteration costs two E-steps.
    (Verified by re-coding these steps by hand: identical results.)
  - BFGS: a general-purpose optimizer (R's optim). It knows nothing
    about mixtures; it just maximizes the log-likelihood function.
  Cost is measured in likelihood evaluations / E-steps, which is fair
  across methods (each costs about the same).

Slide 6.1  Same start, same answer, different price
  Script: compare.R, Figure 1  [MAIN]  + Table 7
  "Convergence race from the same starting point": distance to the
  best log-likelihood vs cost (both axes log scale).

  What to say:
  - From the k-means start all three reach the same maximum
    (-1034.002, same parameters to 3-4 decimals).
  - Cost: EM 18, mixtools 41, BFGS 125 evaluations.
  - Both EM variants improve at every step. BFGS stays flat for a
    long time and then drops suddenly.

  Script: compare.R, Figure 3  [BACKUP]
  "Cost to reach the optimum": bar chart of 18 / 41 / 125 (same
  numbers as Table 7 - use it instead of the table if you prefer).

  Script: compare.R, Figure 2  [SKIP or appendix]
  "Parameter estimates by method": all methods agree so closely that
  the x-axis labels overlap and are unreadable. Table 7 says the same
  thing more clearly.

Slide 6.2  The paths on the likelihood surface
  Script: compare.R, Figure 4  [MAIN]
  "Paths of each optimizer on the likelihood surface": the (mu1, mu2)
  plane with the log-likelihood as colours, start = star,
  end = circle, from a deliberately poor start (means 50 and 60).

  What to say:
  - Two bright peaks = the same solution with the labels swapped
    (mu1 = 55, mu2 = 80 or the reverse).
  - EM and mixtools walk smoothly up to the peak, almost the same
    path.
  - BFGS stops halfway (mu1 ~ 52, mu2 ~ 71): from this start it gets
    stuck and never reaches the peak.

Slide 6.3  Robustness from 100 random starts
  Script: compare.R, Figure 6  [MAIN]  + Table 8
  "Success rate from random starts".

  What to say:
  - Same 100 random starting points for every method.
  - Reached the global maximum: EM 100%, mixtools 100%, BFGS 66%.
  - Median cost: EM 35, mixtools 67, BFGS 280.
  - EM and mixtools need almost the same number of iterations
    (34 vs 33), but mixtools pays 2 E-steps per iteration, so it costs
    about twice as much. Its extra E-step does not speed it up here.
  - mixtools never had to abandon a start because of a collapsing
    variance (0 restarts).

  Script: compare.R, Figure 5  [BACKUP]
  "Where does each method end up from 100 random starts?": final gap
  to the best log-likelihood per run. EM and mixtools are all at the
  bottom; BFGS is spread out, a third of its runs end far away.

  Script: compare.R, Figure 7  [BACKUP]
  "Cost from random starts": boxplots of the cost per run (same
  ranking: EM < mixtools < BFGS).


=====================================================================
PART 7. CONCLUSIONS (final slide)
=====================================================================

  1. EM fits a 2-component mixture to Old Faithful: 36% short waits
     (~55 min) and 64% long waits (~80 min). AIC and BIC both
     confirm 2 components.
  2. The log-likelihood never decreases - confirmed in every run of
     every experiment.
  3. EM only finds a local maximum and can get stuck at a saddle point
     or collapse a component: use several starting points.
  4. EM converges linearly. It is fast when the components are well
     separated (r = 0.43) and extremely slow when they overlap
     (r -> 1), because then the labels are uncertain.
  5. With well-separated components EM estimates are accurate and
     classification is almost optimal. With strong overlap the
     estimates are poor, the second component cannot be detected by
     AIC/BIC, and the bootstrap CIs are too narrow.
  6. Compared with other methods: EM is the cheapest and most
     reliable here. mixtools (actually ECM) gives the same answer at
     twice the cost; BFGS is ~8 times more expensive and misses the
     global maximum in a third of the runs.

  Limitations (one line each, good for questions):
  - Only one-dimensional data and normal components.
  - Simulations only use equal weights (0.5/0.5) and equal sds;
    unequal ones were not tested.
  - The bootstrap uses a warm start and an iteration limit, which
    likely contributes to the low coverage with overlap.
